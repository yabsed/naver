Preparing ballots for only 50% of the electorate in Seoul’s Songpa-gu on election day during last June’s local elections was a critical misstep that severely undermined the core values of participatory democracy.

The National Election Commission (NEC) must remain an independent constitutional body, free from the influence of both the legislature and the executive branch. Dismantling the NEC would merely render the election-governing body more partisan than it is today, ultimately compromising the fairness of our elections. 

Rather than solely relying on logical arguments to refute conspiracy theorists' concerns regarding electronic vote-counting machines, we must actively alleviate their anxieties by hiring more election officials and reverting to more analog systems. Achieving this will inevitably require a larger government budget. Furthermore, to guarantee transparent and error-free operations, the government and the National Assembly must institutionalize significantly stricter audits and mandate a much higher degree of information disclosure from the NEC.

To be clear, the recent local elections were not fraudulent. Nevertheless, if public trust in the NEC deteriorates further, citizens will grow increasingly anxious about the reliability of well-intentioned systems like early voting, which are designed to foster greater democratic participation. Should this happen, the victorious party will face constant challenges to its legitimacy, while the defeated party will undoubtedly allege election fraud. A surge in extremist factions refusing to accept election outcomes would profoundly damage the most vital tenet of democracy: the peaceful transfer of power.

We are currently in the final golden window of opportunity to reform the NEC. If we fail to act, might we witness violent unrest unfold in South Korea following the next general elections two years from now?
